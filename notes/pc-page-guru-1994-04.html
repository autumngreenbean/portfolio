    This essay criticizes the term 'multimedia' used by competing corporate industries, citing it "is not a market, it is a technology". Page 54 ... to page 57 demonstrates how to make a title with Expanded Book, a Mac-only software used for making book layouts.

    Section 1: ノリだけで突っ走っていいのか? ("Is it fine to just charge ahead on vibes alone?")

    "Is it fine to just charge ahead on vibes alone?"

    そもそも、マルチメディアなんかに手を出すのは、世界を広げたいからでしょう?

    "In the first place, the reason you get involved in something like multimedia is that you want to broaden your world, isn't it?"

    Section 4: マルチメディアは市場でなく技術である ("Multimedia is a technology, not a market")
    マルチメディア「技術」をどう応用するかはユーザーの自由だ。メーカーやソフトハウスは素材を提供するだけ。その素材を、System 7やWindows 3.1やUNIXの上でどのように組み合わせて使うかは、ユーザーに任されている。

    "How to apply multimedia 'technology' is the user's freedom. Makers and software houses merely supply the materials. How those materials are combined and used on System 7, Windows 3.1, or UNIX is left to the user."

    さて皆さん、マルチメディアは、だいたいこんな感じの世界であります。マルチメディア・タイトルを作るというのもマルチメディアですが、マルチメディア＝マルチメディア・タイトルというワケではないワケです。この世界の中で、あなたはどのマルチメディアをやりますか?

    "Well then, everyone, multimedia is more or less a world like this. Making a multimedia title is also multimedia, but it is not the case that multimedia equals multimedia titles. Within this world, which multimedia will you do?"

    Everyone has a habit or two they just can’t cure. Some people may seem to have no such habits at all, but really they just don’t show up around other people, and who knows what they do when they’re completely alone. Then again, nobody can point out habits like that, and the person may not even notice them. Habits are a perfect way to understand a person. People say you should cure bad habits, but if curing one meant you could no longer tell who that person was, maybe there’s no need to be so insistent about it. For one thing, the person doesn’t feel the habit is bad. So even I never thought of curing mine. My habit is whistling. If I’m walking alone, I start whistling before I know it. I keep whistling on and on, a dubious melody nobody has ever heard, in a sloppy, made-up phrasing. When I was in junior high, my mother caught me coming home whistling and scolded me, “You shouldn’t whistle at night.” But this one habit still won’t go away, even now that I’m thirty. That day too, I apparently was whistling as I walked [along the underground passage in Shinjuku Station toward the subway platforms]. It must have been the usual off-the-cuff, careless tune. Suddenly I noticed a tall man in glasses and a black fedora walking right next to me. And this suspicious-looking man kept glancing at me.

    Like human thoughtThis time I'd like to take up Fundamental Papers in Information Science! (Keiso Shobo), a translated collection of papers on library and information science. But in fact, this book includes Vannevar Bush's paper "AS WE MAY THINK" (Memex; first published in the Atlantic Monthly, July 1945). Incidentally, from the 1920s into the 1930s Bush was a vice president of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, head of the Carnegie Institution, and chairman of the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (the predecessor of NASA). Afterward, as a science advisor to President Franklin D. Roosevelt, he held enormous sway over the White House and was the top scientist of his day. [unclear] He is also famous as the scientific leader of "MJ-12," the American government's top-secret UFO research project familiar from UFO files. A virtual machine to support human thoughtBe that as it may, since his MIT days Bush had kept sounding the alarm about the harm of meaningless rote memorization in science education of the time. And Bush, who loved tinkering with machines since childhood, wanted to build a machine that would take over the rote memorization work, which has almost nothing to do with real thinking, so that people could spend more time on the thinking and creating that is truly theirs. Pull quote: "The MEMEX that Bush proposed is the origin of all media technology." (Vannevar Bush, "Fundamental Papers in Information Science," Keiso Shobo)
    So the result of his twenty-odd years of thinking was the paper "AS WE MAY THINK," written on the theme of the possibility of a virtual machine to support human thought. Bush wrote this paper just after the success of the first atomic bomb test of the Manhattan Project, which he himself had overseen and which had brilliantly led America to victory, and it was published in the Atlantic Monthly. The concept of the virtual machine MEMEXNow, this paper is made up of eight chapters, but what made his name immortal is, after all, the concept of the virtual machine called "MEMEX" (short for "EXtension of MEMory") that appears in chapter 6. He took as an example the tragedy of Mendel's laws of heredity, which lay buried unnoticed for a century, and declared that the techniques for sailing the "information galaxy" were still no different from the navigation techniques of the Age of Exploration, and he presented the concept of the MEMEX machine as a breakthrough. On the other hand, it was precisely this MEMEX that kept inspiring the infonauts who brought about the astonishing progress in media technology, especially in the late '80s. It moved MIT professor J. C. R. Licklider, patron of the infonauts of the '60s, to establish the "Information Processing Techniques Office" at the Advanced Research Projects Agency. Douglas Engelbart of the Stanford Research Institute created "NLS" with ARPA-IPTO funding. Seeing it, the "high-tech Rasputin" Alan Kay
    conceived the "Dynabook" and built the "Alto" prototype at Xerox PARC. Inspired by this in turn, Apple's CEO Steve Jobs developed the "Macintosh." Ted Nelson conceived "hypertext" and "Xanadu," and Robert Taylor built "ARPA-net" (the predecessor of the Internet), which networked the infonauts he had cultivated in the ARPA-IPTO era. The origin of media is hereThat is, in the eighth and final chapter of "AS WE MAY THINK," he even sings of the possibility of "cybermedia" that would communicate directly and interactively with the human brain's neural network system. In other words, the history of media technology progress from the '80s to the present all began with this paper (and MEMEX). Out in the world, the media industry is as loud as ever about "virtual reality" this and "multimedia" that, but there is nothing inside any of it. So I'd like you readers first to read this paper "AS WE MAY THINK" and by all means get a feel for MEMEX, the origin of media. That's my hope. As of now, although most of the technological predictions Bush made in "AS WE MAY THINK" have been realized, only the virtual machine MEMEX itself has yet to appear before our eyes……
